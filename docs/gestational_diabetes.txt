Key points
- Gestational diabetes is a type of diabetes that develops during pregnancy in women who did not already have diabetes.
- Every year, 5% to 9% of pregnancies in the United States are affected by gestational diabetes.
- Managing blood sugar during pregnancy protects both the mother and the baby from complications.

Causes and symptoms
During pregnancy, the placenta produces hormones that can make the body's cells less responsive to insulin (insulin resistance). All pregnant women experience some insulin resistance late in pregnancy, and gestational diabetes occurs when the pancreas cannot produce enough extra insulin to keep blood sugar in a healthy range. Gestational diabetes typically has no noticeable symptoms, which is why routine screening is essential.

Screening and diagnosis
Doctors normally test pregnant women for gestational diabetes between 24 and 28 weeks of pregnancy using a glucose screening test followed, if needed, by an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Women with higher risk factors—such as prior gestational diabetes, obesity, PCOS, or a family history of diabetes—may be tested earlier.

Managing gestational diabetes
- Eat healthy, balanced meals with controlled carbohydrate portions distributed evenly across the day.
- Stay physically active with safe, doctor-approved activities such as walking or swimming.
- Monitor blood sugar levels regularly as directed by your care team.
- Do NOT try to lose weight during pregnancy; instead, work with your doctor on healthy pregnancy weight gain goals.
- If healthy eating and activity are not enough to keep blood sugar in target range, a doctor may prescribe insulin or other pregnancy-safe medication.

Long-term health after pregnancy
Blood sugar levels usually return to normal soon after delivery. However, about 50% of women who have gestational diabetes go on to develop type 2 diabetes later in life. To catch changes early and lower risk:
- Get tested for diabetes 6 to 12 weeks after your baby is born.
- If the test is normal, continue getting screened every 1 to 3 years.
- Maintain healthy eating, regular physical activity, and a healthy postpartum weight.
