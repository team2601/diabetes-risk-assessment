Insulin Resistance & Prediabetes
https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/prediabetes-insulin-resistance
What is insulin resistance?
Insulin resistance occurs when cells in your muscles, fat, and liver do not respond well to insulin and cannot easily take up glucose from your blood. As a result, your pancreas makes more insulin to help glucose enter your cells. As long as the pancreas can produce enough extra insulin to overcome this weak response, blood glucose levels remain in a healthy range.

How insulin resistance leads to prediabetes and type 2 diabetes
Over time, if cells remain resistant and the pancreas can no longer keep up with the increased demand for insulin, blood glucose levels begin to climb.
- First, blood glucose rises above normal into the prediabetes range (A1C 5.7% to 6.4%, or fasting glucose 100 to 125 mg/dL).
- Without intervention, continued insulin resistance and declining insulin production can progress to type 2 diabetes.

Causes and risk factors for insulin resistance
While the exact cause is not completely understood, key contributors include:
- Excess body weight, especially visceral fat stored around the abdomen (belly fat)
- Physical inactivity, since active muscles burn glucose and respond much better to insulin
- Genetic factors and family history of type 2 diabetes
- Older age, high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol and high triglycerides, sleep apnea, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Certain medications, such as long-term glucocorticoids (steroids)

Signs and associated metabolic changes
Insulin resistance and prediabetes usually have no obvious symptoms. Some people may develop darkened, velvety patches of skin on the back of the neck, armpits, or groin (acanthosis nigricans). Insulin resistance often appears alongside high triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol, and elevated blood pressure.

Can insulin resistance be reversed?
Yes. Increasing physical activity and losing a modest amount of excess weight (5% to 7% of body weight) help muscle and liver cells respond to insulin more effectively again, often lowering blood glucose back toward normal ranges and preventing type 2 diabetes.
