Key points
- Managing diabetes every day can affect emotional well-being, and mental health directly influences blood sugar and self-care.
- Depression, anxiety, and diabetes distress are common among people with diabetes and are treatable with the right support.

How diabetes affects mental health
- Depression: People with diabetes are 2 to 3 times more likely to experience depression than people without diabetes, yet only 25% to 50% of people with diabetes who have depression get diagnosed and treated.
- Anxiety: People with diabetes are about 20% more likely to experience anxiety at some point in their lives. Because symptoms of anxiety (such as sweating, trembling, fast heartbeat, and dizziness) closely overlap with symptoms of low blood sugar (hypoglycemia), always check your blood sugar if you are unsure which one you are experiencing.
- Diabetes distress: Feeling overwhelmed, frustrated, discouraged, or burned out by the constant daily demands of diabetes care is called diabetes distress. Between 33% and 50% of people with diabetes experience diabetes distress during any 18-month period.

How stress and mental health affect blood sugar
Chronic stress raises stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline, which can push blood sugar levels higher. Emotional exhaustion can also make it harder to plan meals, stay active, check blood glucose, or take medications consistently.

Getting support and coping strategies
- Talk openly with your doctor or diabetes care team about how you feel—mental health care is a core part of diabetes care.
- Ask for a referral to a mental health counselor experienced in chronic health conditions.
- Join a Diabetes Self-Management Education and Support (DSMES) program or peer support group so you do not manage diabetes alone.
- Break self-care into one or two small manageable goals at a time, stay physically active, and practice daily stress-relief habits.
